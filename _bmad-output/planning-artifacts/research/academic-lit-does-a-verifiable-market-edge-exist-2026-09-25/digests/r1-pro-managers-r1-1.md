# Digest r1-1: Professional active managers and hedge funds

**Dimension:** Do professionals with large resources beat passive benchmarks net of fees, and can skill be separated from luck?
**Accessed:** 2026-09-25 (all sources). **Budget used:** 16 research calls, 10 sources.

**How the sources were read.** spglobal.com and Wiley returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch. The SPIVA figures and most journal abstracts therefore come from search-engine extracts of the publisher's own page or abstract. Only Barras, Scaillet & Wermers (2010) was read in full text: the published JF PDF on Wermers' UMD page, which states the journal volume and pages in its header. Confidence is lowered wherever a claim rests on an extract.

## Bottom line for the decision

- **Net of fees, the typical professional does not beat the benchmark.** About 92% of US domestic equity funds underperformed over 20 years to 2025 (SPIVA). After controlling for false discoveries, about 0.6% of US equity funds had truly positive net alpha by 2006 (BSW 2010).
- **Skill before fees is real but rare, and fees absorb it.** 9.6% of funds show skill before expenses against 0.6% after (BSW). The average fund adds about $3.2M a year of value, and managers take it as fees and assets under management (Berk & van Binsbergen 2015).
- **Whether a skilled minority exists depends on the test.** The Fama–French bootstrap finds skill only in the extreme tails before costs. The Kosowski et al. bootstrap finds a "sizable minority" with persistent net alpha. Harvey & Liu (2022) show the first method has too little power and the second rejects the null too often. The size of that minority is therefore still unsettled.
- **Hedge funds.** Top-fund alpha survives a bootstrap and persists at annual horizons (Kosowski, Naik & Teo 2007). But investors' dollar-weighted returns are 3–7% a year below buy-and-hold, and investor-level alpha is about zero (Dichev & Yu 2011). Database biases add roughly 1.4% a year from backfill and about 3–6% a year from survivorship.

### Berk & Green (2004): not retrieved, stated as unverified belief

Berk & Green, "Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets," *Journal of Political Economy* 112(6), 2004, peer-reviewed. The model: investors supply capital competitively and managerial skill has decreasing returns to scale. Flows then chase past performance until expected net alpha to investors is zero. Skill exists, but managers capture it through fees and size, so persistence in net returns is not expected.

This summary is **not evidenced by a source retrieved this run.** It is only indirectly supported: BSW 2010 (claim 4) calls its 75% zero-alpha result "consistent with the Berk and Green equilibrium," and Berk & van Binsbergen 2015 (claim 7) finds skill reflected in size rather than alpha.

## Answers by question

1. **SPIVA (2025 edition, within the 12-month freshness bar):** about 92% of domestic funds lag over 20 years, and 79% of large-cap funds lagged in 2025. Top-quartile persistence is poor. It is near-zero for most categories over five years (2% for small-cap), and for large-cap it was at or below random over the horizon S&P reports. Claims 1–2.
2. **Luck vs skill:** Fama & French (2010) find few funds cover their costs, with skill in the extreme tails only before fees. BSW (2010) find 0.6% skilled net and 9.6% gross, and the skilled share falls over time. Kosowski et al. (2006) find a sizable minority with persistent skill. The main recent update, Harvey & Liu (2022), shows that Fama–French lacks power and Kosowski et al. overrejects. Claims 3–6.
3. **Berk & van Binsbergen (2015):** the average fund adds about $3.2M a year, skill persists up to 10 years, and it is paid as fees and size. Berk & Green (2004) is stated only as an unverified belief. Claim 7.
4. **Hedge funds:** top-fund alpha persists at annual horizons (Kosowski, Naik & Teo 2007), but investors' dollar-weighted alpha is about 0 (Dichev & Yu 2011). Database biases are about 1.4% a year for backfill and about 3–6% a year for survivorship (low confidence). Getmansky et al. and the trend in hedge-fund alpha over time were not retrieved. Claims 8–10.
5. **Size of the skilled minority:** the best-evidenced estimate is BSW's 0.6% of US equity funds with positive net alpha (2006), against 14.4% in 1990. Before fees the figure is about 10%. Kosowski et al.'s "sizable minority" rests on a method that Harvey & Liu (2022) show overrejects. The SPIVA persistence data are consistent with a very small or zero net-skilled minority among large-cap funds. No retrieved source identifies *who* holds persistent net alpha, beyond statistical tails.
