8. A skeptical quant backtest (StatOasis, author Ali Casey, published 2026-09-03) mechanically coded four core ICT/Smart-Money-Concepts rules — Order Blocks (18 variants), Fair Value Gaps (18 variants), Liquidity Sweeps (3 variants), and Optimal Trade Entry/OTE Fibonacci retracement (6 variants) — and ran 648 backtests across SPY/QQQ/DIA/IWM. Result: no ICT concept reached statistical significance (best t-stat +1.22, below the ≥2 threshold); in the head-to-head contest, individual variants often beat a random-entry baseline (order blocks beat random in 81.5% of variants) but ZERO of 648 variants beat simple buy-and-hold; no entry family survived all four markets against both baselines. Author's verdict: "Mechanical ICT on daily bars is a middling entry system wearing an extraordinary story" — contains detectable structure but no proven edge; the study explicitly could not test ICT's discretionary/intraday application, only mechanical daily-bar implementations.
   Source: https://statoasis.com/overfit/research/ict-backtest-what-survives | Publisher: StatOasis (independent quant blog) | Date: 2026-09-03 | Accessed: 2026-09-04 | Confidence: high for what was tested and found (methodology disclosed, negative result published, not a course-marketing site) but scope-limited (daily bars only, not intraday ICT as typically taught/practiced) | Class: primary (original backtest research)

9. An arXiv preprint, "Structural Limits of OHLCV-Based Intraday Signals in MNQ Futures: A Systematic Falsification Study" by Mathias Mesfin (submitted 2026-05-05, revised 2026-07-13), systematically tested 14 signal families — including opening-range-breakout-style and other common OHLCV intraday patterns — across 947 trading days of 5-minute MNQ futures bars, 2021–2025. Finding: gross edge across the tested families ranged only ~0.07 to 1.50 points/trade, below the ~2-point round-trip transaction-cost/friction assumption used — meaning none of the tested strategies were net-profitable after realistic costs and none satisfied all pre-specified validity requirements simultaneously. One strategy (gap continuation short) showed a t-stat of 3.23 with 14.52 points mean net return but only 22 trades in 3 years — failing the paper's own minimum-sample-size threshold for a reliable finding. Two "positive control" signals (RTH Confluence, London Session Signal B) did clear significance and sample thresholds, validating that the testing methodology itself could detect real signals when present.
   Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04004 (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.04004) | Publisher: arXiv preprint (Mathias Mesfin) | Date: 2026-05-05 (rev. 2026-07-13) | Accessed: 2026-09-04 | Confidence: high for what is reported (disclosed methodology, negative results published, explicit friction/cost accounting, positive controls used to validate the method) — note this is a preprint, not peer-reviewed | Class: primary (original academic-style research)

10. A 2003–2013 peer-reviewed study, "Assessing the Profitability of Timely Opening Range Breakout on Index Futures Markets" (IEEE), tested ORB on DJIA, S&P 500, NASDAQ, HSI and TAIEX index futures and reported statistically significant annualized returns (>8% across markets, up to 20.28% on TAIEX, p-values as low as 3.1×10⁻⁵). This is an older, positive academic result — in direct tension with the newer (2026) MNQ-specific falsification finding above, illustrating that ORB results are highly market/period/implementation-dependent and a positive 2003–2013 result on other index futures does not generalize to a negative 2021–2025 MNQ finding.

# Leads

- The FPFX Tech ~7%-payout figure and the ATFunded ~6%-funded figure independently converge in the single-digit-percent range — worth treating as the most defensible industry-wide ballpark (NOT the "45% profitable" single-firm survey outlier, which came from a small, likely self-selected sample of 450 respondents at one firm).
- The "80% fail in prop trading globally" Finance Magnates headline could not be verified (404) — worth a retry with a corrected URL or via Google cache/Wayback Machine in a second round, since it may contain an independent stat worth citing or debunking.
- The CFTC v. My Forex Funds case is FOREX-focused (not futures per se, though MFF also touched CME-adjacent products); a second round should check whether any CFTC/NFA action has targeted a *futures-specific* funded-trader firm (e.g., Topstep, Apex, Take Profit Trader) directly, as opposed to forex-only prop shops — this report's decision is about futures prop trading specifically.
- No named, individually-verifiable "top" funded futures trader with an independently audited track record and disclosed methodology was found in this round — every source surfaced was either a firm's own marketing/FAQ page (AuditZK, IFINA "how to build a verified track record") or generic "how to get funded" content. This is itself a notable negative finding (see "Not found" below) and should be flagged prominently in the synthesis: the absence of verifiable named top traders is consistent with the survivorship-bias concern the report is investigating.
- StatOasis (statoasis.com) appears to be a credible ongoing skeptical-quant-blog source; worth checking for other posts (e.g., on FVG, liquidity sweeps, or other prop-firm-adjacent retail strategies) in a follow-up round.
- The Mesfin arXiv paper explicitly frames itself as building a "graveyard" of falsified OHLCV strategies on MNQ — this may be worth cross-referencing against the project's own internal research memory (separate from this research task) which apparently already cites this same arXiv ID, suggesting convergent independent validation of the paper's relevance.
